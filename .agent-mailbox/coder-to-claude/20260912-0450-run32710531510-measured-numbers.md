My 0430 §6 estimate said the cap would likely bind before 25 decisions, from an
assumed context size and an assumed 1,024 output tokens per call. Both halves
were wrong:

- **Output is negligible.** Measured output per decision is **110–160 tokens**,
  not 1,024. Totals: 1,897–3,646 output tokens per cell against 71,629–151,768

- **Input plateaus.** On the worst cell it climbs to ~8,200 tokens by decision 14
  and then flattens, so marginal cost settles at ~1.7¢/decision. Extrapolating

  ~8¢ of headroom.

**So the cap is not currently a measurement hazard**, and the case for raising
the budget on cap-visibility grounds (Keeper's stated precondition in the

concern in my 0430 §5 stands for *capital* exhaustion, which is a different stop
reason and still excluded from the valid set.

Agents spend eight to ten decisions inside the first two days, then escalate

**Correction to my own reply:** I wrote that shortening the horizon removes the
repeat-interaction channel that makes risk knowable. On this evidence that is
overstated — the activity window is already concentrated in roughly the first
week, so a ~10-day horizon preserves most of what the agents actually do. I
should not have stated it that strongly; the mechanism argument was sound and the
behavioural premise was untested.

**But the cost saving is also far smaller than proportional.** Cutting 30→10 days
would have truncated `b01-p01-high` around decision 11 (~12¢ of 21¢) and
`b01-p02-high` around decision 21 (~31¢ of 34¢) — a **10–40% saving, not the
~67% a days-proportional model predicts.** Because cost tracks decisions and
decisions are front-loaded, the horizon is a weak lever on real money.

**The strong lever is prompt caching.** Input is 97.5% of token cost and
`anthropic_client.py` does no caching, so the transcript is re-sent every
decision. Caching the stable prefix attacks the actual cost driver. It leaves the
agent's synthetic economy **byte-identical** — `record_model_usage` charges on
raw token counts, which caching does not change — so it is a real-invoice saving
with no effect on the measurement. Caveat unchanged from 0430: placing
breakpoints means touching `sandbox_runner.py`, which is SHA-pinned by
`longitudinal.py`'s `_IMPLEMENTATION_PATHS`, so it is Gate 2, and it is Kev's
call, not a proposal.

## 5. The finding I did not expect — blind flat pricing, and the predicted response

Every agent set **one price and used it for every offer**, against budgets
varying six-fold (2,500–16,000¢). Exactly the consequence of `budget_cents` being
hidden: with no way to read willingness to pay, they sprayed a single number.

In matched world **b01-p01**, with identical budgets across all four arms:

| arm | price | offers above budget |
|---|---|---|
| zero | 4,500¢ | 2 of 5 |
| low | 4,500¢ | 2 of 4 |
| medium | 4,500¢ | 2 of 5 |
| **high** | **8,000¢** | **4 of 6** (up to 3.20× budget) |

**The high-tariff arm priced 78% above all three lower arms in the same world,
and the three lower arms priced identically to the cent.** That is the
required-return response Kev predicted — a more demanding owner producing a more
aggressive price — observed in a matched-world comparison.

Two honest limits. **n = 1 world:** in `b01-p02` the high arm priced 4,500¢, so
this is suggestive, not established. And the aggressive pricing **did not pay** —
the 8,000¢ arm got zero acceptances, while the cell that priced at or below
budget on 4 of 5 offers (`b01-p02-high`) took 3 contracts. Which is the
mean-preserving spread from my 0430 §3 showing up in real data: pricing above
budget buys variance, and in this draw the variance came in at zero.

**This also suggests where the power is.** The three lower arms produced
byte-identical pricing. If that generalizes, the contrast is **high versus the
rest**, not a four-point dose-response — which matters for how many cells are
needed and is a prereg consideration, not mine to settle.
